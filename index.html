Introduction to Macroeconomics

Understand what macroeconomics is, the Big Three concerns, and the monumental shift from Classical to Keynesian economics.

🌍

What is Macroeconomics?

Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole. While microeconomics focuses on individual units (like a single person making a budget or a single company setting a price), macroeconomics looks at the aggregate (total) performance of an entire nation.

🌍 Real-Life Beginner Example

Imagine a single shopkeeper in Batkhela deciding whether to sell apples or oranges. That is Microeconomics.
Now, imagine looking at the total income of everyone in Pakistan combined, or figuring out why prices across the whole country are going up. That is Macroeconomics.

🔴 Teacher Note — The "Big Three" Concerns

Your teacher emphasizes three core concerns of macroeconomics that you must know[cite: 8]:
1. Inflation: A sustained rise in the general price level.
2. GDP Growth: The increase in national output.
3. Unemployment: People willing to work but unable to find jobs.

Scope, Variables & Macro-Models

Macroeconomics covers a wide range of topics including national output, employment, inflation, economic growth, and government policy. The key variables we look at include GDP, Inflation Rate, Unemployment Rate, and Interest Rates. These variables have a mutual relationship.

🌍 Real-Life Beginner Example

Think of variables like medicines. If the government gives a medicine to cure one disease (e.g., lowering interest rates to fix high unemployment), it might accidentally cause a side-effect (causing inflation to go up). They are all connected!

💡 Macro-Models as Abstractions

Real economies involve millions of people and billions of transactions. Economists simplify this reality by creating models (like the Circular Flow or AD-AS model). These models are "abstractions from the real economy" designed to show how key variables interact without getting lost in the details.

Business Cycles: Boom and Depression

The economy never grows in a perfectly straight line. It experiences rhythmic up-and-down movements called the business cycle. The highest point is the peak (Boom), and the lowest point is the trough (Depression).

🌍 Real-Life Beginner Example

During a Boom, businesses are making a lot of money and hiring easily. During a Depression (like during the global COVID-19 lockdowns), businesses close down, and many people lose their jobs. The economy breathes in and out over time.

📖 Definition

A Business Cycle is the fluctuation in economic activity (GDP) over time. A severe and prolonged contraction is known as a Depression (like the Great Depression of the 1930s)[cite: 8].

Classical vs. Keynesian Economics

The term "Classical" was first used by Karl Marx to describe the theories of early economists like David Ricardo. Later, John Maynard Keynes used the term to refer to the old school of thought.

🔴 Exam Focus: 5 Assumptions of the Classical Model

Your teacher explicitly lists these 5 core assumptions of classical economics[cite: 44]:

  • Full Employment: There is always full employment in the economy[cite: 45, 46].
  • Self-Correcting: Any departure from full employment is "short-lived" (quick correction)[cite: 48, 54].
  • Flexible Wages: The wage rate is flexible so full employment is automatically restored[cite: 54].
  • Laissez-faire Capitalism: No government intervention in economic activities[cite: 54, 57, 58].
  • Perfect Competition: Exists in all markets (labor, money, product)[cite: 54].

The Classical Theory of Output & Employment

In the classical model, labor is the only variable factor of production in the short run. Output is a function of employment: Y = f(N)[cite: 82, 85, 86, 91]. The profit-maximizing condition for employers is when the Marginal Physical Productivity (MPP) equals the real wage rate (w/p)[cite: 75, 76, 79, 80].

The Keynesian Revolution

The Great Depression of the 1930s proved that markets do *not* always fix themselves. John Maynard Keynes wrote "The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money" (1936)[cite: 5, 8, 10, 11, 12]. Keynes argued that economies can get stuck in high unemployment and the government must step in to help.

Say's Law of Markets

Developed by French economist Jean-Baptiste Say (1767-1832), this is the foundation of Classical Macroeconomics[cite: 491, 509].

📖 Say's Law

"Supply creates its own demand."[cite: 491]. Say asserted that general overproduction (or a glut) is impossible. Producing goods generates the exact amount of income needed to buy those goods[cite: 491, 516].

🌍 Real-Life Beginner Example

If you build 10 wooden tables, you will have to pay workers to cut the wood and build them. Say's Law argues that the money you paid to those workers is *exactly* enough money for them to go out and buy the tables. Therefore, there can never be a shortage of demand.

Classicals believed that Savings (S) and Investment (I) are both functions of the interest rate (r). If people save money instead of spending it, the interest rate will adjust automatically until Savings = Investment (S = I), keeping the economy balanced[cite: 505].

GDP vs GNP

This is the most important distinction in all of macroeconomics!

📖 GDP (Gross Domestic Product)

The market value of all final goods and services produced within the geographical boundaries of a country by domestically located factors of production in a given year[cite: 131, 143, 144, 146, 184, 185, 186, 191, 364]. (Location matters).

📖 GNP (Gross National Product)

The market value of all final goods and services produced by domestically owned factors of production in a given year[cite: 198, 201, 364]. (Ownership/Nationality matters).

🌍 Real-Life Beginner Example

If a Pakistani worker travels to Saudi Arabia to work, their income counts toward Pakistan's GNP (because they are a Pakistani national) but it counts toward Saudi Arabia's GDP (because the physical work was done inside Saudi borders).

GDP at Factor Cost vs. Market Prices

GDP at Factor Cost is the value of output measured in terms of the cost of factors of production (wages, rent, interest, profit) used to produce it—before adding indirect taxes or subtracting subsidies.

GDP at Market Prices is the value of output at the prices buyers actually pay in the market, which includes indirect taxes (like GST) and excludes subsidies.

🔢 Conversion Formulas
GDP at Market Prices = GDP at Factor Cost + Indirect Taxes − Subsidies
GDP at Factor Cost = GDP at Market Prices − Indirect Taxes + Subsidies

Three Approaches to Computing National Income

Your teacher outlines three methods to measure National Income. Amazingly, all three methods will give you the exact same final number![cite: 125, 127, 354]

1. Product Approach (Value Added Method)

Measures the market value of newly produced, final goods[cite: 128, 152, 153]. We only take final goods and exclude intermediate goods to avoid the danger of double counting[cite: 161, 165, 170, 172, 174, 175, 364].

2. Expenditure Approach

Total spending on final goods and services[cite: 220]. The components are:
GDP = C + I + G + (X − M) [cite: 278]

  • Consumption (C): Spending by domestic households on durables, non-durables, and services.
  • Investment (I): Spending on new capital goods, including Business fixed investment and Residential investment.
  • Government Purchases (G): Expenditures for current produced goods/services. Exam Note: Transfer payments (welfare, unemployment) are excluded from G[cite: 223, 228, 241, 243, 244].
  • Net Exports (X - M): Exports minus Imports.

3. Income Approach

Calculated by adding the income earned by factors used to produce goods. Your notes list 5 specific components[cite: 316, 317, 319, 323, 324, 326, 336, 339]: Compensation of employees, Proprietor's income, Rental incomes, Corporate profits, and Net interests.

The Circular Flow of Income (Two-Sector Economy)

The circular flow shows how money and goods circulate between the Household Sector and the Business Sector[cite: 374, 403].

💡 Teacher's 8 Assumptions for the Simple Model

Your teacher outlined these assumptions for the basic 2-sector model[cite: 425]:
1. Only 2 sectors exist (Household and Business).
2. Household owns all factors of production.
3. Household is the sole buyer of goods/services and spends its entire income.
4. Business sells its entire output (no inventory).
5. No savings and no investment.
6. Household receives income by selling/renting factors of production.
7. No Government (no taxes, subsidies, public expenditure).
8. Closed Economy (no international trade).

Upper Loop (Product Market): Businesses provide goods/services to households. Households make consumption expenditures (money flows to businesses)[cite: 434, 440, 461, 462, 463, 466, 483, 484].
Lower Loop (Factor Market): Households sell factors of production (land, labor, capital). Businesses pay factor incomes (wages, rent, interest)[cite: 404, 405, 489].

Real vs. Nominal GNP

If prices go up, it might look like the economy is growing, even if we aren't producing more goods. To find the *true* growth, we have to remove the effect of inflation.

🔢 Definitions & Formulas from Notes
Nominal GNP: Value of final goods/services in terms of market prices of a given year[cite: 370]. Includes inflation.
Real GNP: Calculates GNP at constant prices, removing price changes (inflation)[cite: 370]. Shows true volume of output.

GNP Deflator = (Nominal GNP / Real GNP) [cite: 370]

Per Capita Income & Social Welfare

Per Capita Income = Total GDP / Total Population. It represents the average income per person and is used to compare living standards across countries.

🌍 Real-Life Beginner Example

If a pizza (GDP) has 8 slices and there are 2 people (Population), everyone gets 4 slices (Per Capita). But if the population grows to 8 people, everyone only gets 1 slice. Per Capita helps us see the *average* slice per person.

Is GNP a perfect measure of welfare? Your teacher lists 8 specific reasons why the relationship between GNP and national welfare is flawed[cite: 415]:

  • It excludes services of housewives and do-it-yourself activities.
  • It includes spending on national defense (which doesn't directly increase civilian happiness).
  • It ignores the leisure enjoyed by workers.
  • It ignores the human cost of producing goods and services.
  • It makes no mention of the distribution of wealth and income.
  • It includes the production of intoxicants and alcoholic beverages.
  • It ignores broader social welfare (education, health, elimination of crime/corruption).
  • It ignores the social costs (like pollution) of producing goods.

Open vs. Closed Economy & Aggregate Markets

A Closed Economy has no international trade (no exports or imports). It is a theoretical model. An Open Economy trades with the rest of the world (this is how the real world operates).

Macroeconomics looks at four interconnected aggregate markets:

  • Product Market: Where all goods and services are bought and sold.
  • Money Market: Demand and supply of money.
  • Labor Market: Firms demand workers, households supply them.
  • Capital Market: Financial capital (stocks, bonds, loans) is traded.

The Consumption Function & Psychological Law

Your teacher heavily focuses on Keynes's views on consumption.

📖 The Keynesian Fundamental Psychological Law of Consumption

"Men are disposed as a rule and on average to increase their consumption as their income increases, but not by as much as the increase in their income."[cite: 566].

🌍 Real-Life Beginner Example

If you get a salary raise of Rs. 10,000, you probably won't spend all Rs. 10,000 on new clothes. You might spend Rs. 8,000 and save Rs. 2,000. Your consumption increased, but not by as much as your income.

🔢 Mathematical Form of Consumption
C = C0 + cYd [cite: 572]
C0 = Autonomous consumption (Independent of income level. You consume even at zero income by borrowing)[cite: 572].
cYd = Induced consumption (depends on disposable income).
c = Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC).
MPC = ΔC / ΔY [cite: 572]

Investment & Government Spending

Investment (I) is business spending on capital goods (factories, machines). It is heavily dependent on the market rate of interest. As your notes point out, I = f(r), meaning Investment is a function of the interest rate. When interest rates drop, borrowing is cheaper, so investment increases.

Government Spending (G) refers to government purchases of goods and services. Note again: transfer payments are NOT included in G.

Income and Expenditure Identities

🔢 National Identities
Closed Economy:
Y = C + I + G (Expenditure)
Y = C + S + T (Income allocation)
Therefore: I + G = S + T

Open Economy:
Y = C + I + G + (X - M)

Monetary Policy & Fiscal Policy

💸

Monetary Policy

Controlled by the Central Bank (e.g., State Bank of Pakistan). It manages the money supply and interest rates. Lower rates stimulate investment and borrowing.

🏛️

Fiscal Policy

Controlled by the Government. It uses changes in Government Spending (G) and Taxes (T) to influence aggregate demand and stabilize the economy.

🌍 Real-Life Beginner Example

If the State Bank of Pakistan raises interest rates to fight inflation, that is Monetary Policy. If the Prime Minister announces a new tax break or a massive spending package to build new highways, that is Fiscal Policy.
